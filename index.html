How it works

In plain English

  • This week reads Wikipedia's daily news log, tags each item with themes found in prophecies, and asks whether this week has more of those themes than a normal week since 2016.
  • Check an event compares something you describe with 30 prophecies. Match strength (0–100) is high only when the event shares themes and concrete details with a well-sourced text.
  • A match is never proof. Old texts can be made to fit almost anything after the fact. Only predictions written down in advance can test foresight; that is what the Predictions page tracks, including monthly forecasts the index makes about itself, scored after the fact.
  • Nothing here uses AI or sends your text anywhere. It is a fixed set of rules you can read below and argue with.

The details: every number comes from a fixed formula. None is a probability, and none says a prophecy is true.

Two questions, kept apart

Theme overlap asks: does this event look like what the text describes? Almost any war resembles the Four Horsemen. That is worth knowing, and it says nothing about foresight.

Match strength asks: does the event line up with the text's concrete details, from a source we can trust? It measures resemblance, not predictive power, which is why it is not called a prediction score.

The formulas

ComponentRangeHow it is computed
Negation scopeper clauseYour text is split into clauses. Words after a negation or denial cue (no, not, never, without, deny, reject, rule out…) and words before a dismissal (…is false, …was debunked) are set aside and never count toward a match. Emphatic forms such as "not only" and "never before" are not treated as negation.
Event themes0–1 eachThe remaining words are normalized and stemmed, then matched against 23 theme word lists. Each theme's strength is 1 − e−0.9·hits, counting each distinct term once.
Theme overlap (T)0–100100 × (0.65 × cosine similarity between the event's themes and the prophecy's theme weights + 0.35 × min(1, prophecy keywords found ÷ 3)).
Concrete details (S)0–10020 × the prophecy's specificity rating (1–5) × (0.15 + 0.85 × share of its concrete markers found in your text). This measures how specifically the event matches the text: a precise prophecy matched only by theme scores low, and a vague text can never score high.
Source strength (E)20–10020 × the prophecy's evidence rating (1–5). Fixed per prophecy; see the rubric.
Points against (P)0–4515 for each contradiction triggered by your text, plus 6 for each stated precondition your text does not mention. Capped at 45.
Match strength0–100(T ÷ 100) × √(S × E) − P, floored at 0.

Verdicts: theme overlap under 20 is no real match; match strength under 20 is a loose resemblance; 20–39 is some overlap; 40 or more is a close match. Even a close match is found after the fact, never a fulfilment.

The weekly theme index

The index reads Wikipedia's Current Events portal, a volunteer-edited daily log of world news. Each bullet is one item, scored on its own with its topic line as context. Items are never merged into one block of text, so a long day cannot inflate a score.

  • Share: for each theme, the fraction of a period's items that carry it. Shares, not counts, so busier editing years do not look more apocalyptic.
  • Week: the seven days ending on the selected date, pooled. Single days are too noisy with 15–40 items.
  • Baseline: separate, non-overlapping calendar weeks from 1 January 2016 (or an earlier start date, if the history has been extended) to 31 December 2025, so no news day is counted twice. A percentile of 95 means 95% of baseline weeks had a lower share. (Until the first full rebuild after this change, the shipped data still uses overlapping weeks; the page says so when that is the case.)
  • Running high: above that theme's own 90th percentile and carried by at least two news items that week. The two-item rule stops near-silent themes, where a normal week has none, from being tipped over by a single item. With 23 themes, two or three run high in a typical week by chance, so one high theme means little.
  • Themes running high at once: ranked against the same count in real baseline weeks. This, not any single theme, is what makes a week unusual. Weeks next to each other are still related (a war lasts more than a week), so a rare count may come from a single long cluster of events.
  • Month check: the same test at the scale of a calendar month: a theme is high if its monthly share beats its own 90th percentile across baseline months and it appears in at least three items. Months never overlap, and the check runs on the monthly table shipped with the site, so it needs no rebuild. Themes that have been rising for years clear an old baseline easily, and the page flags when that is part of the answer.
  • Matching item: a news item whose best prophecy match has match strength 20 or more.

Rising, perennial, or just feels new

For each theme we take its share of each year's news (full months only) and sort it into one group:

  • Rising for years: the robust trend (the median of all year-to-year slopes, so one extreme year cannot drive it) rises at least 4% of the theme's average per year, the last three years average at least 1.4× the first three, and at least three of the last four years sit above the 10-year median.
  • Fading: the mirror image.
  • Comes in waves: otherwise, if its busiest month is 8× a normal month or more, or it swings widely from year to year.
  • Always with us: otherwise, if its yearly share stays steady (varies by less than 40% of its average).
  • Too rare to tell: under 0.2% of all items.

Eleven yearly points are few, and a "rising" theme may partly reflect how Wikipedia's editors log the news. Treat these as strong descriptions of this record, not laws of history.

What the index cannot tell you: Wikipedia's editors choose what to log, coverage varies by region and year, the theme word lists are hand-built, and themes like war, hunger and displacement tend to rise together. Ten years is also too short to call anything unprecedented in history. An unusual week is unusual in this record, by these rules.

Forecasts and scoring

Matching old texts to new events can never test foresight, because the match is made after the fact. So ESCHATON also makes forecasts before the fact, about the one thing it measures: its own news index.

  • What is forecast: for each theme averaging at least 0.5% of the news, whether next month's share will be above that theme's median month; and whether three or more themes will run high at once.
  • Three models compete. Base rate: how often the event happened in past months. Momentum: the same, split by whether the theme was above its median the month before. Momentum + convergence: further split by whether three or more themes were running high at once. If convergence meant something, the third model would win.
  • Scoring: the Brier score, the average of (forecast − outcome)², where the outcome is 1 or 0. Zero is perfect; always saying 50% scores 0.25. Probabilities are smoothed counts, (hits + 1) ÷ (cases + 2), so no forecast is ever 0% or 100%.
  • Backtest: every month from January 2019 was forecast using only earlier months, then scored. The page recomputes this live from the shipped data.
  • Live forecasts: issued in the last days of each month for the next month, with the current month to date as the starting state, then written to the registry before the month begins. They are scored automatically once the month's news is in.
  • What would refute it: after 12 live months, if momentum's Brier score is not below the base rate's, the momentum claim is wrong; if convergence does not beat momentum, convergence has no predictive value. The backtest already points to the second.

The control test

Does the matcher find something specific to prophecies, or would it "find" matches in anything? To check, ESCHATON builds 30 decoy prophecies from shuffled parts of the real ones: theme weights moved to random other themes, keywords drawn at random from the whole pool, concrete markers borrowed from other prophecies, and the same evidence and specificity ratings. Every news item is scored against the decoys as well as the real texts. If decoys match about as often, the matches are an artefact of the method. The result appears under the index once the data has been rebuilt.

The public registry

  • Append-only chain: data/registry.json holds every forecast, registered prediction and outcome. Each entry's fingerprint is the SHA-256 hash of its content plus the previous entry's fingerprint. Changing, deleting or reordering anything breaks every fingerprint after it, and this page checks the whole chain in your browser.
  • Independent timestamps: each new chain head is also stamped with OpenTimestamps, which anchors the hash in the Bitcoin blockchain for free. Anyone can check a proof with the ots verify tool. Git history is a third record.
  • Outcomes: recorded later as separate entries that point to the original, never by editing it.
  • Who can register: the repository owner and collaborators directly; anyone else's issue is registered when a maintainer adds the register label, so the registry can't be spammed.
  • Drafts in this browser carry a timestamp and fingerprint too, and keep earlier fingerprints when edited. But you control your own browser, so a draft proves nothing to anyone else until it is registered.

Longer history

Two free ways to look back further than 2016. The Wikipedia ingest accepts an earlier start date; older daily pages are patchier, and missing days are skipped rather than guessed. For decades, the optional New York Times Archive API (free key) adds monthly theme shares from headlines and abstracts back to 1945 or earlier. That series is a different lens, so it is shown separately and only compared with itself, decade against decade.

Ratings are our judgments, with a written reason on every record. They are meant to be argued with; edit data/prophecies.json to change them.

Prediction results

  • Pending The resolve-by date has not passed.
  • Supported The outcome met criteria that were stated before it happened.
  • Failed The resolve-by date passed without the criteria being met.
  • Inconclusive The criteria are vague or contested, the claim has no date, or the record may have been written after the event.

Sourcing rules

  • Every record names a work, a locator (chapter, verse, line, hadith or reading number) and an edition or translation.
  • Quotations come from public-domain editions (KJV, Bellows, Evelyn-White, original Latin and French) or are kept under fifteen words. Everything else is labelled as a paraphrase.
  • Where a tradition is oral, contested within its own community, or known only from a late record, the record says so and the evidence rating reflects it.
  • Links point to reference editions or overviews for checking, not to endorsements.

Known limits

  • Capitalized words inside a sentence are treated as names, so "Mark Carney" or "Miami Heat" do not trigger the archetypes for mark or heat. Generic event words such as Hurricane, War or Outbreak still count when capitalized.
  • Matching reads words, not meaning. Negation is handled by clause-level rules, which catch plain denials ("officials deny…", "there is no…", "claims of… are false") but not sarcasm, hypotheticals ("could", "if"), or a denial reported by someone who is not credible.
  • Scores on Check an event have no baseline of their own; This week supplies one for news as a whole.
  • Any large enough set of vague texts will contain a match for any event. The specificity and evidence terms exist to discount exactly that.
  • Thirty texts are a sample. Absence from the database means nothing about a tradition.

ESCHATON is a comparative study tool. It treats every text with the same rules and endorses none of them. Static HTML, CSS, JavaScript and JSON; no backend, no paid APIs.
